How it worksThe study routine

Preparation should have a sequence.

Arthic is being designed to connect the pieces students often have to manage separately: the exam, the practice, the explanation and the plan for what comes next.

The platform is in development. The steps below explain the intended experience, not features you can use today.

01

Start with the exam you're taking.

Choose your route first—MDCAT, ECAT, NUST NET, FAST, LUMS Law, CSS or PMS. Each exam has its own format and preparation needs, so the starting point should be specific to that test.

The aim: keep your preparation connected to the exam and the date you're working towards.

02

Work on one topic at a time.

Move between focused practice and past-paper preparation without having to organise everything from scattered files. Work through a topic, then return to the parts that still feel uncertain.

The aim: make each session about a defined piece of learning, not just the number of questions completed.

03

Understand the reason behind a mistake.

The planned mentor experience is built around a simple loop: identify where the reasoning went off track, explain the concept in a clearer way, and give you a new question to see whether the idea has stuck.

The aim: learn from the step that went wrong, instead of only seeing that an answer was incorrect.

04

Know what deserves attention next.

The planned study tools bring topic review, your exam date and the next study session into one place. The point is to make it easier to return to what needs work, not to give you another dashboard to maintain.

The aim: leave a session with a sensible next step.

The mentor's three stepsPlanned approach

A wrong answer should teach you something.

When a question exposes a gap, the intended mentor response follows the same three stages.

01

Identify

Find the point where the reasoning breaks: the wrong formula, a missed condition, a definition or a step in the logic.

02

Explain

Revisit the concept in plain language and use a different explanation when the first one hasn't made sense.

03

Test

Try a new question that checks the same underlying idea, so the next step is based on understanding rather than reassurance.

Illustrative example

One physics mistake. Three useful steps.

A student is solving a projectile-motion question and chooses an equation that requires time, even though the question gives displacement instead.

Identify

The missing time value is the issue. The student has selected a relation that needs information the question does not provide.

Explain

Revisit the relationship between velocity, acceleration and displacement, and why a different equation fits the information given.

Test

Try another question with a different set of values but the same underlying reasoning.

This is a written example of the proposed teaching approach—not a live mentor conversation or a released product screen.
